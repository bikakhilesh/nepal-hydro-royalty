What the billing data confirms

Four checks the monthly billing data holds up against on its own: the seasonal shape of generation and price, an age-based rate threshold, a decade of fleet growth, and a listed company’s own reported revenue. Switch between them below.

Output and price move in opposite directions

Nepal's fleet is overwhelmingly run-of-river, so generation tracks the monsoon: it peaks in Aswin and falls through the winter to barely a fifth of nameplate by Falgun. The power purchase tariff is built to counteract exactly that, paying a steep dry-season premium in the four months when the rivers are lowest.

A 70% dry-season premium. The tariff holds near NPR 4.94/kWh from Baisakh through Mangsir, then jumps to exactly NPR 8.40/kWh for Poush, Magh, Falgun and Chaitra — the four months when median capacity factor sits between 0.22 and 0.33.

The fifteen-year threshold, verified against the register

The register carries a commercial operation date for every licensed plant. Joining those dates to the assessments turns the pattern above into a testable claim — and it holds with a sharpness rarely seen in administrative data. Below age 15 essentially no plant pays the high capacity rate; from age 15 essentially all of them do.

A fleet that quadrupled in a decade

Recorded generation runs from about 3.1 TWh across 29 plants in 066/67 to 14.5 TWh across 188 plants in 082/83. Royalty assessed and royalty received track each other closely — the collection side of this system is, on the whole, current.

The pipeline behind that growth

Every plant carries a licence date as well as a commissioning date, so the register also records the development pipeline: when the state granted permission, when each project actually delivered, and the years in between.

A lag you can see.
Read 073/74–075/76 with care. Recorded generation dips there while installed capacity keeps rising. That is a reporting gap, not a collapse in output: only 34 of 62 plants filed monthly detail in 073/74. Those bars are drawn faded, and the royalty series — which comes from a different, more complete part of the page — shows no such dip.

The register reproduces a listed company’s revenue

The royalty base is the plant’s energy invoice, and that invoice is the operating company’s top line. Mapping register billing months onto the Shrawan–Asar accounting year for Likhu-4 (52.4 MW, Green Ventures Co. Ltd, commissioned 2078/07/21) reproduces its reported Income from Sale of Energy quarter by quarter — six of eleven quarters land within 0.01%, on figures of NPR 270–570 million.

Two adjustments make it line up. The register files each fiscal block Asar–Jestha, one month ahead of the Shrawan–Asar accounting year, so the window has to be shifted; and the published quarterly figures are cumulative year-to-date, so they have to be differenced.
Where it breaks is informative. The −20.7% quarter is Baisakh 2081, carrying the register remark “बिल रकम बराबर short supply penalty कायम गरेको ।” — a short-supply penalty equal to the whole bill. The register books settlement net of the penalty (zero); the accounts recognise revenue gross on accrual. The gap is a difference in basis, not in data.

Where the fleet actually sits

The register carries a district for most plants but no usable coordinates — every populated latitude field holds the same value, an unedited form default pointing at Illinois. Locations here come from OpenStreetMap and Wikidata where a plant can be matched by name, and from its district otherwise. The distinction is drawn on the map, because it matters: a filled dot is a surveyed position, a hollow one is only district-accurate. The rivers and peaks behind them are the reason the fleet sits where it does.

Dashed dots are not plant locations. They sit at their district’s centroid, spread apart just enough to stay countable, so the cluster shows how many plants a district holds — not where any one of them is. Half-filled dots are on the correct watercourse but at an arbitrary point along it.

05

Method and caveats

How this was collected

The register’s energy-detail page is server-rendered: a plain GET carrying a fiscal-year and a plant identifier returns the full table. No login, no JavaScript, no token. Every plant × fiscal-year combination is fetched and the HTML tables parsed directly. A second read-only pass over the plant register supplies the commercial operation date, licence, owning company and district behind every plant. The current two fiscal years are re-read daily, since receipts post continuously; the full history monthly, which is what catches a correction to a closed year.

Royalty reconciles as Previous Due + Energy + Capacity − Received = Balance to the paisa, which is the main check that the parse is faithful.

Five things worth knowing
  1. Two table schemas. The site serves either Billed Amount to NEA or Invoice to NEA (NRS) depending on the plant; a third variant reports in USD. These are coalesced into one revenue column.
  2. Annual filings. Some NEA-owned plants file a whole year as one row rather than twelve, producing impossible monthly capacity factors of 7–10×. Those 103 rows are excluded from seasonality but kept in annual totals.
  3. Footer bleed. The page's summary block sits inside the same HTML table as the data; 5,876 such rows were removed by requiring a yyyy/m stamp.
  4. Two fields are unedited defaults. Every populated latitude/longitude in the register reads 39.7817,−89.6501 (Springfield, Illinois), and “Province 1” is carried by 97 plants including many demonstrably in Bagmati or Gandaki. Both are ignored; districts, which cross-check correctly, are used instead.
  5. Balance is a stock, not a flow. It carries forward, so balances must not be summed across years. The explorer shows each plant's most recent year only.
